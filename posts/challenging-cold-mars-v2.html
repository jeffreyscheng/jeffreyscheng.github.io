How many wet years did Mars experience? Analyses of eroded Martian craters suggest up to 100M. Professional rock-whisperers say no more than 10M. I think the rock-whisperers are mistranslating slightly using outdated math and that a Martian history of 100 million wet-years is actually plausible.

Ancient Mars experienced liquid erosion. We see canyons and pebbles. Canyons are carved by rivers. Pebbles on Earth can be smoothed by either wind or water, but ancient Mars is known to have had a thin atmosphere since we see tiny meteorite craters (tiny meteorites would have burned up in a thick atmosphere before impact). So pebbles are also strong evidence of liquid water.

We don’t just know that liquid erosion carved Mars’ surface; we even have estimates of the total water flow over the millennia. But it is not easy to distinguish whether Mars was:

  • really wet for a short time
  • a bit wet for a long time.

Two methods give us very different answers:

  1. Simulation: modeling the fluid dynamics of various ancient erosion regimes to see which is most compatible with camera images of modern Mars. insert citation estimates up to 100M “wet years”.
  2. Rock-thermometer: using spacefaring Martian feldspars as a time-traveling thermometer. Shuster & Weiss (2005) ↗ estimate at most 10M “wet years”.

I was upset by how different those numbers are. Berkeley geochronologist Jack Carter and I suspected the discrepancy comes from the 21-year-old Shuster et al result “reading” the rocks as a thermometer using a very underpowered Bayesian model. Using HMC enabled by modern autodiff, we find that 100M wet years is plausible with the rock-thermometer method, resolving the apparent contradiction.

Both ways to estimate the number of “wet years” begin with a meteorite crash.

The rock-thermometer method depends on Earth scientists bringing an ancient Martian feldspar to a lab. This happens when a violent meteorite impact ejects Martian crust matter into space and some falls into Earth orbit by chance. We will discuss the fate of our happy little Martian rock later.

The simulation method uses the lips of craters of various ages. If Mars experienced a few flood years, you’d expect a sharp divide between:

  1. pre-flood craters with very eroded lips
  2. post-flood craters with pristine crispy edges

Instead, we observe a very smooth continuum of increasingly eroded craters. Simulation link citation suggests up to 100M total wet-years.

how to use a rock as a time-traveling thermometer

we already know how old the rock is

Everything below depends on a number we do not have to guess. Nakhla is about 1.3 billion years old. That age was not read off the argon we are about to discuss. It came from other isotope systems entirely — samarium into neodymium, rubidium into strontium, uranium into lead — measured on many nakhlite samples by many labs. Different parents, different daughters, different decay constants, different laboratories. They agree.

This matters more than it sounds. A single isotope measurement gives you one number. You cannot ask it for both an age and a temperature history; there are not enough numbers to go around. The rock-thermometer method works only because somebody already spent the age.

So read the rest of this section in that order. The age is an input. The argon is the measurement. The temperature is what we solve for.

who pumped argon into martian rocks?

Nobody. The rock makes its own.

Feldspar is an aluminosilicate framework with potassium ions sitting in it. A small fraction of natural potassium is ⁴⁰K, which is radioactive. About once every 1.25 billion years, half of it decays. Roughly a tenth of those decays produce ⁴⁰Ar; the rest produce ⁴⁰Ca.

Argon is a noble gas. It bonds to nothing. Once made, an argon atom just sits in whatever cavity of the framework it was born in — as long as the rock stays cold.

when does the argon clock start?

Not at formation exactly. While the rock is hot, argon escapes as fast as it is made and nothing accumulates. The clock starts when the crystal cools enough to hold on to what it produces, which geologists call closure.

For a lava that erupted and chilled, closure and crystallization happen close enough together that the distinction does not matter, and Nakhla is such a rock. That is the second reason the 1.3-billion-year number is safe to lean on: the systems that measured it close at different temperatures, so if the rock had cooled slowly they would disagree. They do not.

how much argon should be in there

Because we know the age, we can compute the answer before opening the rock. Over 1.3 billion years a fixed fraction of the original ⁴⁰K has decayed, and a fixed fraction of that became argon.

\[\bigl[{}^{40}\mathrm{Ar}\bigr]_{\text{expected}} =b\left(1-e^{-\lambda\Delta t}\right) \bigl[{}^{40}\mathrm K\bigr]_0\]
\(\bigl[X\bigr]_t\) the number of atoms of isotope \(X\) in the rock at time \(t\).
\(\Delta t\) 1.3 billion years. Known, from the section above.
\(\lambda\) the ⁴⁰K decay constant, \(\ln 2/(1.25\,\mathrm{Gyr})\).
\(b\) 0.107: the fraction of ⁴⁰K decays that produce ⁴⁰Ar rather than ⁴⁰Ca.

Turned around, this is the familiar K–Ar clock: measure the argon, divide by the surviving potassium, and read off an age. We are going to run it in that direction too, but not to learn the age. We already have the age. We are going to run it to find out how much argon is missing.

measuring the argon that is actually there

Measuring ⁴⁰Ar is easy. Put the rock in a hot oven and the argon comes out, because it bonds to nothing. A mass spectrometer counts it.

Measuring the potassium is not easy. Potassium sits in the crystal as K⁺, held by the framework's electrostatic potential, and it does not offgas. We need the potassium anyway, because argon alone tells us nothing without knowing how much parent produced it.

The trick is to convert some of the potassium into argon on purpose. Irradiate the rock with fast neutrons and a small fraction of its ³⁹K becomes ³⁹Ar through \({}^{39}\mathrm K+n\rightarrow{}^{39}\mathrm{Ar}+p\). That ³⁹Ar offgasses in the same oven as the ⁴⁰Ar. Since the ⁴⁰K/³⁹K ratio in nature is effectively fixed, ³⁹Ar is a proxy for the potassium we could not measure directly.

One unknown remains: how many neutrons the sample actually saw. Nobody calculates that. Instead an Earth feldspar of known age rides along in the same irradiation package, and its known age fixes the conversion factor \(J\) for both rocks at once.

  • known Earth age + measured Earth ⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar → conversion factor for the shared irradiation
  • shared conversion factor + measured Martian ³⁹Ar → proxy for present Martian ³⁹K
  • known natural ⁴⁰K/³⁹K ratio → proxy for present Martian ⁴⁰K
  • Martian radiogenic ⁴⁰Ar/⁴⁰K ratio → apparent age of the Martian feldspar

Let \(E\) denote the Earth monitor and \(M\) the Martian feldspar. After correcting for atmospheric argon and interfering reactions, \(\Delta t_E\) is known and \(\mathcal R_E,\mathcal R_M\) are the measured radiogenic ⁴⁰Ar / potassium-derived ³⁹Ar ratios. The number that comes out is

\[ \Delta t_M=\lambda^{-1}\ln\!\left[ 1+\left(e^{\lambda\Delta t_E}-1\right) \mathcal R_M/\mathcal R_E\right]. \tag{1} \]

I have been calling \(\Delta t_M\) an apparent age on purpose. Equation 1 assumes the rock kept every atom of argon it ever made. When that assumption holds, \(\Delta t_M\) is 1.3 billion years and we have learned nothing we did not already know. When it fails, \(\Delta t_M\) comes out too young, and the shortfall is the whole experiment.

derive the age expression

For either co-irradiated rock \(i\), define the corrected measured ratio

\[\mathcal R_i= \frac{\bigl[{}^{40}\mathrm{Ar}\bigr]^i_t} {\bigl[{}^{39}\mathrm{Ar}\bigr]^i_t}. \]

The irradiation contributes the same unknown conversion factor \(J\) to both rocks:

\[e^{\lambda\Delta t_i}-1=J\mathcal R_i.\]

The Earth monitor has known age, so its equation fixes that nuisance factor. Dividing the Martian equation by the Earth equation eliminates \(J\):

\[ e^{\lambda\Delta t_M}-1 = \left(e^{\lambda\Delta t_E}-1\right) \frac{\mathcal R_M}{\mathcal R_E}. \]

Substituting the two measured isotope ratios and taking the logarithm gives the expression above.

the missing argon is the thermometer

Argon is loose in the framework. Warm the crystal and an argon atom will hop from cavity to cavity until it reaches the edge of a grain and is gone. The rock keeps producing ⁴⁰Ar the entire time. It just stops keeping all of it.

So compare the two numbers we now have. We know how much argon 1.3 billion years should have produced. We measured how much is left. The ratio is the retained fraction, and it is a direct readout of how warm this rock has been.

This is why the independent age was not optional. If we had to infer the age from the argon, a warm old rock and a cold young rock would look identical, and the whole method would collapse. Knowing the age is what turns a shortfall into a measurement.

It is still only one number, though. A single retained fraction is consistent with a mild warmth lasting a long time or a fierce warmth lasting briefly. To separate those we need the rock to report more than once.

what a retained fraction means, quantitatively

To turn retention into temperature we need a model of how fast argon leaves. I use the one from Lovera, Richter & Harrison (1989) ↗, which everyone in this field uses. Argon leaves by thermally activated volume diffusion, and the diffusivity follows the Arrhenius law:

\[D(T)=D_0\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{R_gT}\right)\]
\(T\) absolute temperature, in kelvin — never °C.
\(D(T)\) diffusivity, in area per time. Larger \(D\) means argon spreads faster.
\(E_a\) activation energy: what a mole of argon needs to squeeze from one site in the framework to the next. Near 117 kJ/mol for Nakhla's feldspar.
\(R_g\) the gas constant, so \(R_gT\) is the thermal energy available per mole.
\(D_0\) the diffusivity in the limit where every attempt clears the barrier.

Take logarithms and the law is a straight line: \(\ln D=\ln D_0-(E_a/R_g)(1/T)\), with slope \(-E_a/R_g\). That is the plot geochronologists actually fit, and it is why the kinetics are always quoted as the pair \((E_a,D_0)\).

Escape also depends on how far the argon has to travel. A spherical domain of radius \(r\) empties at a rate set by \(D(T)/r^2\), so \(D_0\) and \(r\) only ever appear together, and \(r\) enters squared: doubling it makes the characteristic diffusion time four times longer.

We do not know \(E_a\) or \(D_0/r^2\) for this particular feldspar. We measure them with step heating.

the rock reports more than once

The irradiation left ³⁹Ar throughout the same grains that held ³⁹K. In step heating we hold the rock at a sequence of hotter and hotter temperatures and measure the packet of ³⁹Ar released after each hold. Both argon isotopes obey the same diffusion law, so the ³⁹Ar release is a controlled experiment on the kinetics.

The result is a staircase, because the instrument collects one packet per hold. Even a single uniform sphere empties over many steps: argon near the surface has a shorter route out than argon near the centre. A candidate pair \((E_a,D_0/r^2)\) predicts the entire staircase, and fitting that prediction calibrates this feldspar.

show the single-sphere release equation

After step \(j\), the dimensionless diffusive exposure is

\[ \Theta_j=\sum_{i\le j}\frac{D(T_i)}{r^2}\Delta t_i. \]

For argon initially distributed uniformly through a sphere, the cumulative fraction released is

\[ F(\Theta_j)=1-\frac{6}{\pi^2} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-n^2\pi^2\Theta_j}}{n^2}. \]

imperfection is what saves us

A perfectly uniform crystal would be a bad thermometer. One escape distance means one retained fraction, and one number cannot separate a warm short interval from a cool long one.

Real feldspar is not uniform. It is a mixture of diffusion domains with different escape distances. Lovera et al. model this by giving every domain the same \(E_a\) and \(D_0\) and letting only \(r\) vary, so each domain \(i\) has its own \(D_0/r_i^2\), and the rock's release is the volume-weighted sum over them.

Now the same warm interval does different things to different parts of the same rock. A small domain may lose nearly all of its argon while a large one keeps nearly all of it. Step heating separates the domains — low-retentivity ones give up their ³⁹Ar in the early, cool oven steps, high-retentivity ones hold on until the end — so we get a retained fraction from each. That is a spectrum of constraints instead of a single number, and it is enough to pull temperature and duration apart.

Which leaves one modelling choice, and it turns out to be the one that matters: what shape is the distribution of domain sizes?

augmenting shuster et al with modern computational bayesian models

shuster et al's 2-spike model

Shuster & Weiss answered the shape question with two domains. Their predicted release curve is a weighted sum,

\[F_{\mathrm{model}}=\sum_i\phi_iF_i,\qquad\sum_i\phi_i=1\]

where \(F_i\) is the release curve of domain \(i\) and \(\phi_i\) is its fraction of the rock. Their fit put 97% of the volume in a high-retentivity domain and 3% in a low-retentivity one. Writing \(x=\ln(D_0/r^2)\) for effective diffusion scale, that is a distribution made of two point masses:

\[p(x)=\phi\,\delta(x-x_1)+(1-\phi)\,\delta(x-x_2)\]

Two spikes is a strong claim. It says the rock has exactly two escape distances and that we know them exactly. The high-retentivity material is what survives a long warm interval, so the location and weight of that spike translate almost directly into the temperature limit. Fixing it fixes the answer.

I replaced the two point masses with a flexible distribution and carried its uncertainty all the way through to temperature.

the full posterior via NUTS

I divide \(x=\ln(D_0/r^2)\) into 28 bins and infer the fraction of the rock in each. A candidate distribution predicts the laboratory ³⁹Ar staircase. Given a proposed temperature history, and the known 1.3-billion-year production budget, the same distribution predicts the surviving ⁴⁰Ar spectrum. Both predictions have to hold at once.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\theta,T\mid y_{39},y_{40},s) &\propto p(y_{39}\mid\theta)\\ &\quad{}\times p(y_{40}\mid\theta,T,s)\\ &\quad{}\times p(\theta)\,p(T). \end{aligned} \]

Here \(\theta\) holds the diffusion distribution, activation energy and measurement model; \(T\) is the excursion temperature; and \(s\) specifies its duration and start time. The two measurements meet through the same \(\theta\), which is exactly where uncertainty about domain structure becomes uncertainty about temperature.

NUTS follows gradients through the joint posterior, so instead of one best-fitting diffusion model I get the whole set of ones the data permit. That is the entire methodological difference, and modern autodiff is the only reason it is cheap enough to do.

how one run covers 21 timing scenarios

The 21 duration-and-start-time cells are mutually exclusive hypotheses for the same observed rock. The sampler targets their mixture,

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\theta,T\mid y) &\propto p(\theta)\,p(T)\\ &\quad{}\times\sum_s \pi_s\, p(y_{39}\mid\theta)\\ &\quad{}\times p(y_{40}\mid\theta,T,s). \end{aligned} \]

Each draw has a posterior responsibility for every cell. Weighting by those responsibilities recovers the conditional posterior for any one timing scenario.

what the rock actually permits

The posterior does not collapse onto two spikes. It has a broad high-retentivity peak and a small low-retentivity tail. Shuster & Weiss's two locations sit inside those broader regions, so their fit was not wrong — it was one member of a family, presented as the family.

was Mars warm?

Here is the honest version of my answer, which is weaker than I wanted it to be.

Carrying the structural uncertainty through does widen the temperature intervals, and it widens them most for the scenarios that matter. But a 100-million-year warm interval is only reachable at the very edge: for an excursion that long beginning about a billion years ago, the upper end of the central 95% interval lands at +1.8°C. Move that same interval later in Martian history and the upper edge falls back below freezing, to −4.5°C for one ending at the present. Two hundred million years never gets there at all.

So the flexible model does not overturn Shuster & Weiss. It converts a clean exclusion into a marginal one, and only for the oldest and shortest of the long excursions. If Mars had 100 million wet years, this rock says they happened early and barely.

Two limits worth stating plainly. Nakhla records the last 1.3 billion years, so none of this touches the Noachian crater record that the four-billion-year argument rests on; that argument also used ALH84001, which I have not reanalyzed. And the lower half of every temperature interval is prior-dominated — at cold enough temperatures the argon stops caring, the likelihood goes flat, and the median is telling you about my prior rather than about Mars. Only the upper edge is a real constraint.
