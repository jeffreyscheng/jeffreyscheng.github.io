challenging the geologists' cold mars history
with bayesian diffusion models

Cameras tell us Mars was warm for hundreds of millions of years. Rocks tell us Mars was barely ever warm.

The cameras hint at existence of ancient water via erosion. Mars's atmosphere is known to have been thin for the last billion years since we see a consistent history of tiny meteorite craters (they would've burned up before impact if the atmosphere were thicker). So smooth pebbles and canyons are unmistakable signs of liquid water, but they're not strong bounds on how long Mars could've had liquid water. For example, the Grand Canyon on Earth only took 5ish million years to carve.

profile reconstruction
crater-age sequence

Martian craters give us one high lower bound around at least 100 million warm years (above 0°C). Imagine old and young meteorite craters. If there were one big ancient deluge, then no old craters would exist, and all young craters would have sharp lips. If there were one big recent deluge, then we wouldn't see craters at all. But if there were slow water erosion over eons, we'd see lots of craters with varying levels of lip smoothness. That's exactly what we see, with a total duration of at least 100My.

Using some clever forensics, geologists can repurpose silicates like feldspars into time-traveling thermometers. We have feldspars from Mars called Nakhlites, and a 2005 study by Shuster & Weiss (surprisingly) indicates that Mars could not have been warmer than 0°C for more than 10M years in the last billion.

Berkeley geochronologist Jack Carter and I suspected that the 21-year-old Shuster et al result was overconfident, so I revisited the inverse problem with a flexible diffusion model and gradient-based MCMC on eight GPUs. The result is narrower than I expected: Nakhla does not support a recent 100-million-year interval above freezing. An interval that old and beginning near the start of the last billion years reaches slightly above 0°C at the edge of its 95% posterior interval.

how to use a rock as a time-traveling thermometer

who pumped argon into martian rocks?

A feldspar is an aluminosilicate with a tiled tetrahedral backbone. In potassium feldspar, potassium occupies large framework cavities, loosely coordinated by surrounding oxygens rather than built into the tetrahedral backbone. Since Potassium-40 (⁴⁰K) is radioactive, old feldspars will end up with some radiogenic Argon gas trapped inside.

when does the argon clock start?

In the simplest clock model, argon produced before closure is lost. As feldspar cools, diffusion slows until argon is retained on the timescale of interest. I call that crossover \(t_{\mathrm{closure}}\). The diffusion model below treats closure continuously.

how to date a feldspar

After closure, ⁴⁰K disappears at a known rate and radiogenic ⁴⁰Ar accumulates. If we knew the feldspar’s initial ⁴⁰K content, we could date it from either the ⁴⁰K remaining today or the radiogenic ⁴⁰Ar produced since closure. We usually do not know that starting quantity, but it cancels when we divide the daughter count by the surviving parent count.

Let \(N_X(t)\) count atoms of isotope \(X\), and let \(\Delta t\) be the time since closure. ⁴⁰K has decay constant \(\lambda=\ln 2/(1.25\,\mathrm{Gyr})\). Of all ⁴⁰K decays, the fraction \(b=0.107\) produces ⁴⁰Ar; the rest produce calcium. The daughter-to-parent ratio is therefore

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal Q &=\frac{N_{^{40}\mathrm{Ar}}(t)} {N_{^{40}\mathrm K}(t)} =b\!\left(e^{\lambda\Delta t}-1\right),\\ \Delta t &=\frac{1}{\lambda} \ln\!\left(1+\frac{\mathcal Q}{b}\right). \end{aligned}\]

This simple clock assumes that the feldspar began with no radiogenic argon and lost none after closure. The diffusion model below allows later argon loss.

dating without a separate potassium measurement

Measuring ⁴⁰Ar is easy: put the rock in a very hot oven and the neutral argon diffuses out. A mass spectrometer measures the offgassed argon. The same procedure fails on ⁴⁰K. Potassium is present as K⁺, and the feldspar’s electrostatic potential locks those ions in place. We therefore need to infer the age without measuring ⁴⁰K directly.

  • Isotope abundances act like fingerprints. The natural ⁴⁰K/³⁹K ratio is effectively constant here and is known for Martian material. ³⁹K is also not measurable by offgassing, but we can perform a trick.
  • Suppose we irradiate the rock with fast neutrons. A small fraction of its ³⁹K becomes ³⁹Ar through \({}^{39}\mathrm K+n\rightarrow{}^{39}\mathrm{Ar}+p\), and heating can release that ³⁹Ar. The converted fraction is basically a property of how angry the neutron irradiation is: its neutron fluence and energy spectrum. This fraction is unknown and hard to calculate, but we can employ another trick.
  • An Earth feldspar of known age is placed beside the Martian feldspar in the same irradiation package. Their adjacent positions let one unknown conversion factor calibrate both rocks:
    • known Earth age + measured Earth ⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar → conversion factor for the shared irradiation
    • shared conversion factor + measured Martian ³⁹Ar → proxy for present Martian ³⁹K
    • known natural ⁴⁰K/³⁹K ratio → proxy for present Martian ⁴⁰K
    • Martian radiogenic ⁴⁰Ar/⁴⁰K ratio → age of the Martian feldspar

Let \(E\) denote the Earth monitor and \(M\) the Martian feldspar. After correcting for atmospheric argon and interfering reactions, \(\Delta t_E\) is known and \(\mathcal R_E,\mathcal R_M\) are the measured radiogenic ⁴⁰Ar / potassium-derived ³⁹Ar ratios. The Martian feldspar’s age is

\[ \Delta t_M=\lambda^{-1}\ln\!\left[ 1+\left(e^{\lambda\Delta t_E}-1\right) \mathcal R_M/\mathcal R_E\right]. \tag{1} \]
derive the age expression

For either co-irradiated rock \(i\), define the corrected measured ratio

\[\mathcal R_i= \frac{N_{^{40}\mathrm{Ar}}^i(t)} {N_{^{39}\mathrm{Ar}}^i(t)}. \]

The irradiation contributes the same unknown conversion factor \(J\) to both rocks:

\[e^{\lambda\Delta t_i}-1=J\mathcal R_i.\]

The Earth monitor has known age, so its equation fixes that nuisance factor. Dividing the Martian equation by the Earth equation eliminates \(J\):

\[ e^{\lambda\Delta t_M}-1 = \left(e^{\lambda\Delta t_E}-1\right) \frac{\mathcal R_M}{\mathcal R_E}. \]

Substituting the two measured isotope ratios and taking the logarithm gives the expression above.

time-traveling riddle: using a perfect crystal as a thermometer

I claim that we can use a single rock as a time-traveling thermometer for the temperature of the Martian surface, which changed over time. Let’s first consider a much simpler case. An ancient Martian alien walks over to a fresh volcanic eruption, picks up a piece of newly-formed feldspar made of perfectly homogeneous crystal grains, chucks it into a refrigerator held at one constant temperature for 1.3 billion years, and then teleports the aged feldspar into our living room on Earth.

“Perfectly homogeneous” is the useful fiction: every spherical grain has the same radius \(r\), the same \(D_0\), and the same \(E_a\). The rock spends exactly 1300 million years at one unknown temperature. We know how to measure its age and the natural ⁴⁰Ar that survived. Our remaining task is to infer the temperature of the alien’s refrigerator.

Argon’s neutrality explains why it is not locked to a charged lattice site like K⁺, but neutrality alone does not give us a diffusion law. The Arrhenius form follows from a more specific picture. An argon atom spends most of its time rattling inside a stable cage in the solid crystal. Reaching the next cage requires passing through a narrower, higher-energy configuration.

Suppose the lattice remains structurally stable, thermal equilibrium holds, and one effective barrier \(E_a\) dominates the hop. Boltzmann statistics then give the relative probability of reaching the barrier. If \(\nu_0\) is the attempt frequency, the successful hop rate is

\[\Gamma(T)=\nu_0\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{R_gT}\right).\]

Each unbiased three-dimensional hop moves the atom a characteristic distance \(\ell\), so \(\langle x^2\rangle=\Gamma(T)t\ell^2=6D(T)t\). Folding the attempt rate, jump length, geometry, and correlations into \(D_0\) gives

\[D(T)=D_0\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{R_gT}\right)\]
\(D(T)\) argon diffusivity at temperature \(T\)
\(D_0\) pre-exponential diffusivity
\(E_a\) activation energy for diffusion
\(R_g\) gas constant
\(T\) absolute temperature
\(r\) effective diffusion distance through a grain

This is an effective kinetic model, not a claim that every microscopic pathway through feldspar is literally identical. Over the fitted laboratory range, feldspar remains a solid framework and argon is an inert guest rather than part of that framework, so thermally activated cage-to-cage hopping is a reasonable reduction. Step heating supplies the empirical check: one barrier predicts a straight line in \(\ln(D/r^2)\) against \(1/T\). At a low refrigerator temperature, nearly all radiogenic argon remains trapped; at a higher temperature, more escapes.

This experiment is called step heating. The dating experiment has already supplied a laboratory ³⁹Ar tracer. Instead of jumping immediately to the hottest oven setting, we hold the rock at a sequence of known temperatures and measure the packet of ³⁹Ar released during each step. Because ³⁹Ar and ⁴⁰Ar are isotopes of the same element, they follow effectively the same diffusion law.

The figure solves the spherical diffusion equation for one domain under a representative Nakhla-style schedule: five minutes at each successively hotter temperature, with \(E_a=117\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) and \(\ln[(D_0/r^2)/(1\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})]=5.7\). The cumulative curve is a staircase because we collect one measurement after each hold. It does not jump from zero to one in a single step. Argon near a grain’s surface has a shorter escape route than argon near its center, so the middle 80% of the release still spans about 475–750°C in this example.

\[ \Theta_j=\sum_{i\le j}\frac{D(T_i)}{r^2}\Delta t_i, \qquad F(\Theta_j)=1-\frac{6}{\pi^2} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-n^2\pi^2\Theta_j}}{n^2}. \]

Fitting the measured staircase to this curve gives \(E_a\) and the effective diffusion scale \(D_0/r^2\).

We can now replay the rock’s known age under a range of candidate refrigerator temperatures. Each temperature predicts how much natural ⁴⁰Ar should remain. The temperature whose prediction matches the measured ⁴⁰Ar is the inferred temperature of the alien’s refrigerator.

time-traveling riddle: using a heterogeneous rock as a better thermometer

The perfect crystal was deliberately unrealistic. A real feldspar contains diffusion domains with different effective sizes and retentivities. During the same warm interval, a small or low-retentivity domain can lose most of its argon while a large or high-retentivity domain loses almost none.

That heterogeneity makes the inverse problem harder, but it also gives us more than one thermometer. A homogeneous rock supplies one leakage response. A mixture of domains supplies a family of responses at different temperature scales.

Step heating separates those responses in the laboratory. Low-retentivity domains release their ³⁹Ar during the early, cooler oven steps; high-retentivity domains hold on until later, hotter steps. The release curve therefore constrains how much material occupies each diffusion scale. The natural ⁴⁰Ar retained across those same domains then constrains the Martian temperature history.

Shuster & Weiss approximated Nakhla with two domains: 3% low-retentivity material and 97% high-retentivity material. In other words, they replaced the unknown distribution of diffusion scales with two spikes. That is the model I revisit below.

augmenting shuster et al with modern computational bayesian models

shuster et al's 2-spike model

Shuster & Weiss fit Nakhla’s step-heating curve with a weighted sum of domain-specific release curves:

\[F_{\mathrm{model}}=\sum_i\phi_iF_i,\qquad\sum_i\phi_i=1\]

Their two-domain fit assigned 97% of the modeled volume to a high-retentivity domain (HRD) and 3% to a low-retentivity domain (LRD). If \(x=\ln(D_0/r^2)\) is the effective diffusion scale, the model is two point masses:

\[p(x)=\phi\,\delta(x-x_1)+(1-\phi)\,\delta(x-x_2)\]

Two domains are a useful summary of the release curve. For a temperature limit, the exact location and weight of the most retentive material matter enormously. I wanted to carry uncertainty in the whole diffusion-scale distribution through the thermal calculation.

the full posterior via NUTS

I replaced the two point masses with a smooth probability vector over 28 diffusion scales. The laboratory ³⁹Ar release constrains that distribution. The natural radiogenic-argon spectrum constrains temperature under a specified excursion duration and start time.

\[p(\theta,T\mid y_{39},y_{40},s)\propto p(y_{39}\mid\theta)\,p(y_{40}\mid\theta,T,s)\,p(\theta)\,p(T)\]

Here \(s\) is one duration-and-start-time cell. The sampler marginalizes over 21 mutually exclusive cells in one expanded ensemble. It sums their likelihoods; multiplying them would count the same rock spectrum repeatedly. Each draw carries a responsibility for every cell, which recovers the posterior conditional on that cell.

I ran 64 NUTS chains on eight A100 GPUs, with 2,000 adaptation steps and 4,000 retained draws per chain. The maximum rank-normalized and folded \(\widehat R\) was 1.0010. The minimum bulk and tail effective sample sizes were 83,413 and 96,173. There were no divergences or maximum-depth transitions. Every cell had at least 63,341 responsibility-weighted samples.

The animation predates the joint analysis. I could not recycle those samples: the earlier fit normalized by all extracted ³⁹Ar and used a legacy spherical-release approximation. The current fit uses the retained total through 850°C and exact spherical eigenmodes.

Importance reweighting confirms that the old sample does not cover the corrected target: Pareto \(k=3.44\), effective sample size 1.06 out of 25,600, and one draw receives 97.2% of the weight. A fresh posterior sample was necessary.

I ran a corrected laboratory-only control to separate the change in diffusion physics from the addition of the natural argon spectrum. Its maximum \(\widehat R\) was 1.0005, with zero divergences.

The old and corrected targets disagree, as the failed reweighting predicts. The corrected laboratory control and joint posterior agree closely: across six shared summaries and all 21 timing cells, their marginal-density overlap is 93.2–96.0%. The natural spectrum mostly identifies excursion temperature; it does not materially relearn the diffusion distribution.

The release schedule does not identify two exact domain sizes. It permits a broad peak near the high-retentivity domain and a small tail near the low-retentivity domain. was Mars warm?

These are direct temperature posteriors from the joint likelihood. There is no 1%-argon-loss threshold. I used a uniform temperature prior from −100 to 100°C.

For a 100-million-year excursion ending now, the central 95% interval and median are [−94.45, −18.57, −4.47]°C. Only 0.25% of its conditional posterior is above freezing.

The long cold tails are not measurements of a −95°C climate. Below the temperature where diffusion becomes negligible, the rock cannot distinguish one cold temperature from another, so the lower tail follows the prior bound. The upper tail contains the useful constraint.

This Nakhla analysis covers the last 1.3 billion years. It does not test Noachian crater erosion several billion years ago, and it does not replace the ALH84001 half of Shuster & Weiss's four-billion-year argument. My original comparison put those time windows too close together.

The result does not support a recent 100-million-year interval above freezing. A 100-million-year interval near the beginning of the last billion years is not excluded at the central-95% level, but it has little posterior mass above 0°C under this prior.
