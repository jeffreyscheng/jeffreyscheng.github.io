How many wet years did Mars experience? Analyses of eroded Martian craters suggest up to 100M. Professional rock-whisperers say no more than 10M. I think the rock-whisperers are mistranslating slightly using outdated math and that a Martian history of 100 million wet-years is actually plausible.

Ancient Mars experienced liquid erosion. We see canyons and pebbles. Canyons are carved by rivers. Pebbles on Earth can be smoothed by either wind or water, but ancient Mars is known to have had a thin atmosphere since we see tiny meteorite craters (tiny meteorites would have burned up in a thick atmosphere before impact). So pebbles are also strong evidence of liquid water.

We don’t just know that liquid erosion carved Mars’ surface; we even have estimates of the total water flow over the millennia. But it is not easy to distinguish whether Mars was:

  • really wet for a short time
  • a bit wet for a long time.

Two methods give us very different answers:

  1. Simulation: modeling the fluid dynamics of various ancient erosion regimes to see which is most compatible with camera images of modern Mars. insert citation estimates up to 100M “wet years”.
  2. Rock-thermometer: using spacefaring Martian feldspars as a time-traveling thermometer. Shuster & Weiss (2005) ↗ estimate at most 10M “wet years”.

I was upset by how different those numbers are. Berkeley geochronologist Jack Carter and I suspected the discrepancy comes from the 21-year-old Shuster et al result “reading” the rocks as a thermometer using a very underpowered Bayesian model. Using HMC enabled by modern autodiff, we find that 100M wet years is plausible with the rock-thermometer method, resolving the apparent contradiction.

how to use a rock as a time-traveling thermometer

who pumped argon into martian rocks?

A feldspar is an aluminosilicate with a tiled tetrahedral backbone. In potassium feldspar, potassium occupies large framework cavities, loosely coordinated by surrounding oxygens rather than built into the tetrahedral backbone. Since Potassium-40 (⁴⁰K) is radioactive, old feldspars will end up with some radiogenic Argon gas trapped inside.

when does the argon clock start?

In the simplest clock model, argon produced before closure is lost. As feldspar cools, diffusion slows until argon is retained on the timescale of interest. I call that crossover \(t_{\mathrm{closure}}\). The diffusion model below treats closure continuously.

how to date a feldspar

If we know the feldspar’s initial ⁴⁰K content, we can date it from the radiogenic ⁴⁰Ar produced since closure.

\[\Delta t=\frac{1}{\lambda} \ln\!\left(1+\frac{1}{b}\, \frac{\bigl[{}^{40}\mathrm{Ar}\bigr]_t} {\bigl[{}^{40}\mathrm K\bigr]_t}\right)\]
\(\bigl[X\bigr]_t\) the number of atoms of isotope \(X\) in the rock at time \(t\).
\(\Delta t\) time elapsed since closure — the age we are after.
\(\lambda\) the ⁴⁰K decay constant, \(\ln 2/(1.25\,\mathrm{Gyr})\).
\(b\) 0.107: the fraction of ⁴⁰K decays that produce ⁴⁰Ar. The rest produce ⁴⁰Ca, which is why only this fraction of the parent shows up as argon.
\(\bigl[{}^{40}\mathrm{Ar}\bigr]_t\) radiogenic ⁴⁰Ar still held in the crystal. Writing the age this way assumes none of it has escaped, which is an assumption that the rock has stayed cold.
\(\bigl[{}^{40}\mathrm K\bigr]_t\) the ⁴⁰K that has not yet decayed.

dating without a separate potassium measurement

Measuring ⁴⁰Ar is easy: put the rock in a very hot oven and the neutral argon diffuses out. A mass spectrometer measures the offgassed argon. The same procedure fails on ⁴⁰K. Potassium is present as K⁺, and the feldspar’s electrostatic potential locks those ions in place. We therefore need to infer the age without measuring ⁴⁰K directly.

Isotope abundances act like fingerprints. The natural ⁴⁰K/³⁹K ratio is effectively constant here and is known for Martian material. ³⁹K is also not measurable by offgassing, but we can perform a trick.

Suppose we irradiate the rock with fast neutrons. A small fraction of its ³⁹K becomes ³⁹Ar through \({}^{39}\mathrm K+n\rightarrow{}^{39}\mathrm{Ar}+p\), and heating can release that ³⁹Ar. The converted fraction is basically a property of how angry the neutron irradiation is: its neutron fluence and energy spectrum. This fraction is unknown and hard to calculate, but we can employ another trick.

An Earth feldspar of known age is placed beside the Martian feldspar in the same irradiation package. Their adjacent positions let one unknown conversion factor calibrate both rocks:

  • known Earth age + measured Earth ⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar → conversion factor for the shared irradiation
  • shared conversion factor + measured Martian ³⁹Ar → proxy for present Martian ³⁹K
  • known natural ⁴⁰K/³⁹K ratio → proxy for present Martian ⁴⁰K
  • Martian radiogenic ⁴⁰Ar/⁴⁰K ratio → age of the Martian feldspar

Let \(E\) denote the Earth monitor and \(M\) the Martian feldspar. After correcting for atmospheric argon and interfering reactions, \(\Delta t_E\) is known and \(\mathcal R_E,\mathcal R_M\) are the measured radiogenic ⁴⁰Ar / potassium-derived ³⁹Ar ratios. The Martian feldspar’s age is

\[ \Delta t_M=\lambda^{-1}\ln\!\left[ 1+\left(e^{\lambda\Delta t_E}-1\right) \mathcal R_M/\mathcal R_E\right]. \tag{1} \]
derive the age expression

For either co-irradiated rock \(i\), define the corrected measured ratio

\[\mathcal R_i= \frac{\bigl[{}^{40}\mathrm{Ar}\bigr]^i_t} {\bigl[{}^{39}\mathrm{Ar}\bigr]^i_t}. \]

The irradiation contributes the same unknown conversion factor \(J\) to both rocks:

\[e^{\lambda\Delta t_i}-1=J\mathcal R_i.\]

The Earth monitor has known age, so its equation fixes that nuisance factor. Dividing the Martian equation by the Earth equation eliminates \(J\):

\[ e^{\lambda\Delta t_M}-1 = \left(e^{\lambda\Delta t_E}-1\right) \frac{\mathcal R_M}{\mathcal R_E}. \]

Substituting the two measured isotope ratios and taking the logarithm gives the expression above.

Every step above assumed the crystal kept every atom of ⁴⁰Ar it ever made. That assumption is a statement about temperature, and it fails when the rock gets warm. Argon is a neutral atom sitting in a cavity of the aluminosilicate framework; warmth lets it hop from cavity to cavity until it reaches the edge of a grain and is gone. The rock goes on producing ⁴⁰Ar the entire time. It simply does not keep all of it.

The black wedge is what leaves, and its size is exactly what we cannot measure. A young rock that retained all of its argon and an old rock that lost half of it hand the mass spectrometer the same \(\mathcal R_M\), so Equation 1 returns an age that is too young by an unknown amount. Both ⁴⁰Ar and ³⁹Ar diffuse, but the ³⁹Ar was made weeks ago in the reactor and has not had a billion years in which to escape, so the loss does not cancel in the ratio.

If a perfectly uniform feldspar crystal loses ⁴⁰Ar to diffusion, it becomes impossible to date. Fortunately, real rocks are not perfect. We are going to employ a forensic trick on the heterogeneous crystal structure that is so effective that we extract not only the rock’s accurate birth date but also the rock’s past temperature.

time-traveling riddle: using a perfect crystal as a thermometer

I claim that we can use a single rock as a time-traveling thermometer for the temperature of the Martian surface, which changed over time. Let’s first consider a much simpler case. An ancient Martian alien walks over to a fresh volcanic eruption, picks up a piece of newly-formed feldspar made of perfectly homogeneous crystal grains, chucks it into a refrigerator held at one constant temperature for hundreds of millions of years, and then teleports the aged feldspar into our living room on Earth.

We now know how to infer the rock’s age: irradiate it beside an Earth feldspar, bake both rocks, measure the offgassed ³⁹Ar and ⁴⁰Ar, and apply Equation 1. To solve the toy problem, we need two more links: how temperature controls argon’s escape, and how the surviving argon reveals the refrigerator temperature.

Temperature controls how quickly argon moves through the feldspar. I use the diffusion model of Lovera, Richter & Harrison (1989) ↗, the multiple-diffusion-domain (MDD) model that ⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar thermal-history work has used ever since, Shuster & Weiss included. It says that argon leaves a crystal by thermally activated volume diffusion, with a diffusivity \(D\) that depends on temperature through the Arrhenius law:

\[D(T)=D_0\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{R_gT}\right)\]

Reading it symbol by symbol:

\(T\) absolute temperature, in kelvin — never °C. The law is Arrhenius only on an absolute scale.
\(D(T)\) diffusivity at that temperature, in area per time (cm²/s by convention). Larger \(D\) means argon spreads through the crystal faster.
\(E_a\) activation energy, in energy per mole: what a mole of argon needs to break out of its site in the aluminosilicate framework and squeeze into the neighboring one. For Nakhla’s feldspar it sits near 117 kJ/mol.
\(R_g\) the gas constant, 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, so \(R_gT\) is the thermal energy available per mole at temperature \(T\).
\(D_0\) the pre-exponential factor, in the same units as \(D\): the diffusivity in the hypothetical limit where every attempt succeeds.

Grouped that way, the exponent \(E_a/(R_gT)\) is a pure number — the height of the barrier measured in units of the thermal energy available to clear it — and the Arrhenius factor \(\exp[-E_a/(R_gT)]\) is the fraction of attempts that succeed, a number between 0 and 1 that rises toward 1 as the rock warms.

“Arrhenius” is a claim about a straight line. Taking logarithms of both sides,

\[\ln D(T)=\ln D_0-\frac{E_a}{R_g}\cdot\frac{1}{T},\]

so plotting \(\ln D\) against \(1/T\) gives a line with intercept \(\ln D_0\) and slope \(-E_a/R_g\). That plot is what geochronologists actually fit, and it is why the kinetics are always quoted as the pair \((E_a,D_0)\): one is the slope, the other the intercept.

Lovera et al. index the model by the diffusion domain — the region argon must cross to get out — rather than by the grain. A spherical domain of characteristic radius \(r\) empties at a rate set by \(D(T)/r^2\), so the intercept and the radius only ever appear together:

\[\frac{D(T)}{r^2}=\frac{D_0}{r^2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{R_gT}\right)\]

The perfect crystal of this toy problem has a single value of \(D_0/r^2\). The MDD model’s one addition is that a real feldspar holds many domains that share the same \(E_a\) and \(D_0\) and differ only in \(r\); each then has its own \(D_0/r^2\), and the rock’s measured release is the volume-weighted sum over them. Size separates the domains sharply, because \(r\) enters squared: doubling \(r\) makes the characteristic diffusion time four times longer.

The age measurement does not tell us \(E_a\) or \(D_0/r^2\) for this feldspar. We measure them with step heating.

The irradiation used for dating leaves ³⁹Ar throughout the same grains that contained ³⁹K. In a step-heating experiment, we hold the rock at a sequence of successively hotter temperatures and measure the packet of ³⁹Ar released after each hold. ³⁹Ar and ⁴⁰Ar follow the same diffusion law.

The observations form a staircase because the instrument collects one packet after each hold. Even one perfectly homogeneous spherical grain empties over many steps: argon near the surface has a shorter route out than argon near the center. Under the five-minute schedule shown, the middle 80% of the simulated release spans about 475–750°C.

A candidate pair \((E_a,D_0/r^2)\) predicts the entire staircase. Fitting that prediction to the laboratory measurements calibrates the diffusion kinetics of this feldspar.

show the single-sphere release equation

After step \(j\), the dimensionless diffusive exposure is

\[ \Theta_j=\sum_{i\le j}\frac{D(T_i)}{r^2}\Delta t_i. \]

For argon initially distributed uniformly through a sphere, the cumulative fraction released is

\[ F(\Theta_j)=1-\frac{6}{\pi^2} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-n^2\pi^2\Theta_j}}{n^2}. \]

Now replay the rock’s known lifetime in the model. For each candidate refrigerator temperature, the Arrhenius law predicts its diffusion rate; the spherical release model predicts how much natural ⁴⁰Ar survives. The candidate that matches the measured ⁴⁰Ar gives the refrigerator temperature.

time-traveling riddle: using a heterogeneous rock as a better thermometer

The toy rock has one diffusion distance, so every grain responds on the same temperature scale. The multiple-diffusion-domain (MDD) model treats real feldspar as a mixture of domains, each with its own effective argon escape distance. During the same warm interval, a small domain may lose most of its argon while a large domain retains nearly all of it.

Those different responses give us several thermometers inside one rock. During step heating, low-retentivity domains release their ³⁹Ar in the earlier, cooler oven steps; high-retentivity domains hold on until later, hotter steps. The full staircase tells us how much material responds at each diffusion scale.

The natural ⁴⁰Ar retained across those same domains then constrains the Martian temperature history. The remaining modeling choice is the shape of the diffusion-domain distribution.

augmenting shuster et al with modern computational bayesian models

shuster et al's 2-spike model

Shuster & Weiss chose a discrete distribution with two domains. The predicted release curve is their weighted sum:

\[F_{\mathrm{model}}=\sum_i\phi_iF_i,\qquad\sum_i\phi_i=1\]

Here \(F_i\) is the release curve of domain \(i\), and \(\phi_i\) is its fraction of the modeled rock.

The fit assigned 97% of the modeled volume to a high-retentivity domain (HRD) and 3% to a low-retentivity domain (LRD). If \(x=\ln(D_0/r^2)\) denotes effective diffusion scale, this is a distribution with two point masses:

\[p(x)=\phi\,\delta(x-x_1)+(1-\phi)\,\delta(x-x_2)\]

The high-retentivity material controls how much argon can survive a long warm interval, so uncertainty in the location and weight of that part of the distribution matters directly for the temperature limit. I replaced the two point masses with a flexible distribution and carried its uncertainty through the thermal calculation.

the full posterior via NUTS

I divide \(x=\ln(D_0/r^2)\) into 28 bins and infer the fraction of the rock in each bin. A candidate distribution predicts the laboratory ³⁹Ar staircase. Given a proposed Martian temperature history, the same distribution also predicts the surviving natural ⁴⁰Ar spectrum.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\theta,T\mid y_{39},y_{40},s) &\propto p(y_{39}\mid\theta)\\ &\quad{}\times p(y_{40}\mid\theta,T,s)\\ &\quad{}\times p(\theta)\,p(T). \end{aligned} \]

Here \(\theta\) contains the diffusion distribution, activation energy, and measurement model; \(T\) is the constant excursion temperature; and \(s\) specifies its duration and start time. The laboratory and natural-argon measurements meet through the same \(\theta\). This is where uncertainty about the diffusion-domain distribution becomes uncertainty about temperature.

NUTS follows gradients through the joint posterior and leaves samples in proportion to posterior probability. It gives a distribution of plausible diffusion models and temperatures instead of one best-fitting answer.

how one run covers 21 timing scenarios

The 21 duration-and-start-time cells are mutually exclusive hypotheses for the same observed rock. The sampler targets their mixture,

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\theta,T\mid y) &\propto p(\theta)\,p(T)\\ &\quad{}\times\sum_s \pi_s\, p(y_{39}\mid\theta)\\ &\quad{}\times p(y_{40}\mid\theta,T,s). \end{aligned} \]

Each draw has a posterior responsibility for every cell. Weighting by those responsibilities recovers the conditional posterior for any one timing scenario.

I ran 64 NUTS chains on eight A100 GPUs, with 2,000 adaptation steps and 4,000 retained draws per chain. The maximum rank-normalized and folded \(\widehat R\) was 1.0010, where 1 means that the chains agree; the minimum bulk and tail effective sample sizes were 83,413 and 96,173. No transitions diverged or reached maximum tree depth.

The twelve displayed traces overlap and repeatedly cross the same parameter ranges.

why the comet animation and final run use different samples

The exploratory animation used a laboratory likelihood that normalized by all extracted ³⁹Ar and used a one-mode approximation on the long-time branch. The final likelihood uses the retained total through 850°C and converged spherical eigenmodes.

Importance reweighting cannot bridge the two targets: Pareto \(k=3.44\), effective sample size 1.06 out of 25,600, and 97.2% of the weight on one draw. I therefore sampled the corrected posterior directly.

A corrected laboratory-only control separates the information in the two experiments. Its diffusion marginals overlap the joint posterior by 93.2–96.0% across all six summaries and all 21 timing cells.

The laboratory staircase therefore determines most of the diffusion distribution. Adding the natural ⁴⁰Ar spectrum mainly determines which excursion temperatures are compatible with that calibrated distribution.

The posterior does not concentrate at two exact diffusion scales. It has a broad high-retentivity peak and a small low-retentivity tail. The two Shuster & Weiss locations fall within those broader regions, while the NUTS fit preserves the range of distributions allowed by the staircase.

The horizontal axis is \(\ln(D_0/r^2)\), so smaller values indicate slower argon loss. It is an effective diffusion scale rather than a direct measurement of grain diameter.

Sampler diagnostics tell us that NUTS explored its target. Posterior predictive checks ask whether that target can reproduce the two experiments. Fourteen of eighteen scored laboratory points and seventeen of eighteen natural-spectrum points fall inside their 90% replicated-data intervals.

The laboratory misses cluster in the middle of the heating schedule. The likelihood includes a fitted discrepancy term for variation left out of the reported measurement errors, including recoil, alteration, geometry, and heating-duration error.

was Mars warm?

For each timing scenario, the joint likelihood gives a posterior over constant excursion temperature. The plot shows excursions ending at the present under a uniform prior from −100 to 100°C.

Each scenario contains one constant-temperature excursion; the model treats natural argon loss outside that interval as negligible.

For a 100-million-year excursion ending now, the median is −18.6°C and the 97.5th percentile is −4.5°C. Only 0.25% of the posterior lies above freezing. For the same duration beginning one billion years ago, the 97.5th percentile reaches 1.8°C, while 4.5% of the posterior lies above freezing.

The figure is cropped at −40°C because colder values carry little additional information. Once diffusion is negligible, Nakhla cannot distinguish one cold temperature from another; the upper tail supplies the useful temperature limit.

Nakhla records roughly the last 1.3 billion years. These results do not test Noachian crater erosion several billion years ago, and they do not include the ALH84001 meteorite used in the four-billion-year argument by Shuster & Weiss.

Under this prior, discrepancy model, and single-excursion history, a recent 100-million-year interval above freezing lies outside Nakhla’s central 95% posterior interval. An equally long interval near the beginning of the last billion years reaches above 0°C at the edge of that interval, but most of its posterior remains below freezing.
