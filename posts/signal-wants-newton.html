signal wants newton, noise wants whitening

Muon is the fastest LLM optimizer right now, and it is the maximally humble one: it never has enough confidence to apply curvature information. My premise is that signal directions want to be preconditioned by the inverse Hessian and noise directions want to be whitened, so the dial between Newton and Muon should be set by the per-direction SNR. That sounds like it needs an expensive new measurement. It doesn't — in DDP we already compute everything required and then throw it away.

01

muon is a steepest descent optimizer

Muon is the fastest LLM optimizer right now. The benchmark that the optimization community uses is track 3 of the nanogpt speedrun, created by Muon creator Keller Jordan. Muon combines spectral descent (Shampoo with \(\beta_2=0\)) with heavyball momentum. The momentumless update is equivalent to many things:

  • steepest descent under the spectral norm,
  • the polar factor \(UV^\top\) of the gradient \(G=USV^\top\),
  • \(\operatorname{msign}(G)\),
  • preconditioning by the inverse square root of the Gram matrix, \((G^\top G)^{-1/2}G\).

Keller Jordan implemented Muon's msign operation using Newton–Schulz. Muon took off whereas Shampoo didn't for a few reasons: it's very fast — just 15 matmuls and 3% more overhead than Adam — and it "just works." No one tunes Muon.

where the spectral norm comes from

The gradient is referred to as the direction of steepest descent, but this masks a silent assumption. For a vector \(w\in\mathbb{R}^m\), function \(L:\mathbb{R}^m\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\), and arbitrary norm \(\Vert\cdot\Vert_\_\) we define the direction of steepest descent \(\Delta w\) at \(w\) to be

\[\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\Vert \Delta w\Vert_\_=1} \langle \Delta w, -\nabla_w L\rangle.\]

If we constrain \(\Delta w\) to the \(L^2\) hypersphere, we do recover that the unit perturbation of steepest descent is proportional to the gradient. However, this does not hold true of other norms. If we instead constrain \(\Delta w\) to the \(L^\infty\) hypercube, we get \(-\operatorname{sgn}(\nabla_w L)\), the element-wise sign of the vector; this corresponds to the nearest corner on the hypercube where each vertex coordinate is \(\pm 1\). Different choices of norm surface lead to different steepest descent update rules.

So which norm should we pick for LLM optimization? It is known that the kernel regime has poor regularization. We instead want feature learning, where for every layer \(\ell\) with weights \(W_\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\), both activations \(h_\ell\) and gradients \(\nabla_{h_\ell}L\) are of order \(\Theta(\sqrt{n})\). As per Condition 1 from Yang, Simon, and Bernstein's "A Spectral Condition for Feature Learning," we get the feature learning regime (\(\mu P\)) when both the weights \(W_\ell\) and the updates \(\Delta W_\ell\) have spectral norm \(\Theta\!\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)\). The norm that controls feature learning is the spectral norm, so that is the norm whose steepest descent direction we want.

Working it out gives the polar factor. Given weight matrix \(W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\), inner product \(\langle A,B\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(A^\top B)\), spectral norm \(\Vert\cdot\Vert_2\), and loss \(L\), write \(G=\nabla_W L\) with SVD \(G=\sum_{i=1}^r\sigma_i u_i v_i^\top\). The inner product achieved by the direction of steepest descent is bounded by the nuclear norm of the perturbation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \max_{\Vert\Delta W\Vert_2=1} \langle \Delta W, G\rangle &=\max_{\Vert\Delta W\Vert_2=1} \operatorname{tr}\!\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i u_iv_i^\top\right)^{\!\top} \Delta W\right]\\ &=\max_{\Vert\Delta W\Vert_2=1} \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i \operatorname{tr}\!\left(v_i u_i^\top \Delta W\right)\\ &=\max_{\Vert\Delta W\Vert_2=1} \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i\, u_i^\top \Delta W v_i\\ &\leq \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i \;=\; \Vert G\Vert_*. \end{aligned} \]

It's trivial to show that this equality is achieved by \(\Delta W=UV^\top\). Therefore the direction of steepest descent is the polar factor of the gradient, which we notate \(\operatorname{msign}(G)\), and the steepest descent optimizer is uniquely

\[W_{t+1}=W_t-\eta\cdot \operatorname{msign}(\nabla_{W_t} L).\]
02

the premise

Notice what that derivation optimized for: feature learning. Nothing in it mentions convergence, and Muon does not converge even on toy problems — it snaps every singular value to one, so it never takes a short step in a direction it has already solved. Newton's method has the opposite profile. It works near convergence, since the Taylor approximation is best at the bottom of the basin, but it does not learn features well in the infinite width limit.

My premise is that "signal" really wants to be preconditioned by the inverse Hessian (Newton's method), which should lead to convergence in near-quadratic settings. But noise really wants to be preconditioned by the inverse square root Hessian (whitening). Steepest descent methods like Muon and Shampoo do the latter.

Ideally, we implement an optimizer that is equivalent to Newton's method in update directions that are noiseless and equivalent to whitening in update directions that are pure noise. Muon is the maximally humble version of this optimizer, where it never has enough confidence to apply curvature information; Muon happens to be optimal under an isotropic Hessian, where the curvature is the same anyway.

Read that way, the two methods are not rivals but two settings of one dial — which power of the curvature matrix to precondition by. Whitening is the exponent \(\tfrac12\); Newton is the exponent \(1\). If we knew the SNR of each possible update direction, we could straightforwardly decide how much to turn the dial between Newton and Muon, per direction, every step.

03

the empirical update is biased

There is a second, sharper reason to want per-direction noise information, and it is a defect in msign itself rather than a preference about convergence rates.

For true gradient \(G=\nabla_W L\) and minibatch gradient \(\hat G=G+E\) under Gaussian noise model \(E\sim N(0,\Sigma)\), we can straightforwardly show that \(\hat G\) is an unbiased estimator of \(G\) by linearity of expectation. However, \(\operatorname{msign}(\hat G)\) is not an unbiased estimator of \(\operatorname{msign}(G)\).

If full-rank \(G\) and \(\hat G\) differed only in their singular values, we would actually have \(\operatorname{msign}(\hat G)=\operatorname{msign}(G)\), since their singular values get snapped to one anyway. But there will be small perturbations such that the \(j\)-th singular vectors do not exactly align, \(\hat u_j\neq u_j\) and \(\hat v_j\neq v_j\), and so we will be amplifying the wrong singular directions to unit length if we choose \(\operatorname{msign}(\hat G)\) as our update direction. For directions \(u_iv_i^\top\) with extremely low signal — potentially below the noise floor of \(\sigma(E)\) — we should be snapping the singular values to zero; for directions where \(s_i\gg\sigma_{\max}(E)\), we should be snapping \(s_i\) to 1 as before. We assert that there is some optimal noise-aware projection that does better than msign.

This is worth stating as sharply as possible: msign's unit step is not a free normalization. It is a maximally aggressive statement of confidence in the empirical singular basis, applied uniformly, including to the directions that are pure sampling noise. The fix cannot come from the mean gradient, because the mean gradient is exactly the object whose basis is in question. It has to come from a second statistic.

04

the second moment is already free

This sounds hard. It isn't, because the measurement is already sitting in the data-parallel training loop, unused.

In DDP on an 8×H100 with per-GPU batch size \(B\) and effective batch size \(8B\), we get 8 separate microgradients. We currently only use the 1st moment (the mean) when we all-reduce to get the average empirical gradient. But we could also use cross-gradient disagreement (like the second moment) to figure out which directions are signal and which directions are noise. We could figure this out in parameter-space directions or in the spectral basis.

That is the whole proposal: extract the second-order statistic — the covariance — instead of discarding it. The 8 microgradients are independent, equally sized replicas drawn from disjoint data at the same batch size, which is exactly the structure needed to expose the noise level rather than assume it. Their mean is the gradient estimate; their disagreement is the error bar on that estimate; and the ratio of the two, per direction, is the SNR that sets the dial.

The cost accounting is what makes this attractive. Nothing new is computed in the backward pass — the microgradients already exist on each rank, and the all-reduce that averages them already touches every one of them. Nothing new is assumed either, which matters more: a noise model would be a guess about the shape of \(\Sigma\), whereas cross-replica disagreement is a measurement of it. Every departure from Muon's default step should be licensed by a measurement, and this is the measurement we already pay for.

05

the three questions to answer

None of the above is yet an optimizer, and I want to be careful about which parts are premise and which are established. Three questions decide the design, in order:

  • Is balancing curvature-awareness with noisiness even an interesting thing to do?
  • If so, should we work in the raw parameter basis or the spectral basis?
  • Should our noise metric be my proposed spectral echo estimator, or something simpler like a Wiener filter / SNR?

The first question is the one that can kill the whole idea, so it goes first, and it is an empirical question about scale rather than a theoretical one: at the batch sizes and step counts real runs use, is there enough per-direction noise left in the accumulated gradient for any of this to matter? If the answer is no, the honest conclusion is that Muon's humility is already correctly calibrated and the dial should stay where it is. If the answer is yes, then Muon is leaving a measured quantity on the floor at every step, and the interesting work is deciding how much curvature each direction's confidence buys.
